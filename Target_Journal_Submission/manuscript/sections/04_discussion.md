## 4. Discussion

Epoch probabilities of an automatic stager can be turned into per-night intervals for clinical sleep parameters that reach their nominal coverage within a cohort when every component is fitted on the calibration nights alone, for an external pretrained and an in-domain stager. Whether they are more informative than the limits of agreement used in validation studies depended on the parameter. For TST they had lower interval scores than every form of limits of agreement in every cohort, significantly so against distribution-free limits, and allowed more nights to be classified confidently at a 6-h threshold. For the other parameters they offered no consistent advantage, and for WASO in long ambulatory recordings they were worse. Neither the intervals nor limits of agreement transferred reliably between cohorts; calibration on locally scored nights restored average coverage, but not reliably at every site with ten nights.

**Relation to previous work.** Epoch-level uncertainty methods [@kang2021; @fiorillo2021; @phan2022; @heremans2024; @bechny2024; @vainikka2026; @li2025conmil] identify epochs to review, night-level scorability models [@mikkelsen2020; @ganglberger2024] predict whether a recording will be scored well, and Hardarson et al. [@hardarson2023] showed at the population level how staging errors propagate to sleep parameters. Posterior sampling of whole hypnograms has been used to describe the uncertainty of overnight statistics [@vangorp2023icassp; @vangorp2023jbhi] without calibrating it against a scorer. The present procedure calibrates it, in the units clinicians use and for any stager that outputs epoch probabilities, and compares it head-to-head with the agreement statistics that validation studies report [@svensson2024; @beau2025; @kaneda2026].

**Why the gain was specific to TST.** The adaptive scale did what it was designed to do: at a fixed centre, widths proportional to the posterior spread were more efficient than constant widths in most combinations (significantly in {{h_nbetter_yasa_SCALEmu}} of 24 for YASA), and the advantage over limits of agreement was largest on the nights that were hardest to score. Whether this translated into an advantage over limits of agreement depended on how much of a parameter's error the posterior spread explains. TST errors accumulate over the whole night from many ambiguous sleep–wake epochs, which is what sampled hypnograms vary; consistent with this, interval width correlated with the plug-in error for TST in {{e13_spearman_met_TST}} of {{e13_spearman_n_TST}} stager–cohort combinations, but for N2 % in only {{e13_spearman_met_N2pct}} and for WASO in {{e13_spearman_met_WASO}}. Errors in stage percentages are plausibly dominated by systematic differences in how a stager and a scorer draw stage boundaries (for example N3 under different rule sets), which a posterior given the stager's own probabilities cannot see; the posterior spread then adds little to a well-chosen constant width. Latencies depend on single transitions and have skewed error distributions. Regression-based limits of agreement, which model level-dependent bias, achieved competitive interval scores for these parameters but did not reach nominal coverage.

**The WASO failure and the choice of centre.** In SC, the mechanism described in Section 3.6 made the posterior mean over-estimate WASO by {{h_pe_lgbm_sc_s0_w_SC_WASO_ffbs_mean_bias:.0f}}–{{h_pe_yasa_w_SC_WASO_ffbs_mean_bias:.0f}} min and the intervals wide. This is a property of the parameter definition as much as of the method: per-night uncertainty is only meaningful for parameters that are stable to isolated epoch errors, and in clinical PSG ending at lights-on (HMC) the problem was absent. The pre-specified configuration was also not optimal: removing the regulariser of the score lowered the interval score by about {{(1-1/h_meanratio_yasa_norm_ffbs)*100:.0f}} % on average, and the calibrated plug-in was an equally good centre for several parameters. We did not tune the method on these results; parameter-specific choices need confirmation in independent data.

**Implications for practice.** A laboratory deploying an automatic stager can (i) score a set of local nights manually; (ii) use them to recalibrate the stager's probabilities, which by itself removed most of the TST bias; and (iii) report TST with a per-night interval, reviewing nights whose interval is wide or straddles a decision threshold. For parameters other than TST, distribution-free limits of agreement, calibrated on the same local nights, were a simpler alternative that was on average similarly informative (mean interval-score ratio excluding TST {{h_meanratio_noTST_yasa_scp_signed:.2f}} and {{h_meanratio_noTST_lgbm_sc_s0_scp_signed:.2f}}), with parameter-specific exceptions. Ten local nights restored average coverage, but a site's realised coverage still fell below 80 % in a median over parameters of {{h_loc_p80_yasa_HMC_10_norm_reg_ffbs*100:.0f}} % (HMC) and {{h_loc_p80_yasa_ST_10_norm_reg_ffbs*100:.0f}} % (ST) of our repetitions (up to {{h_loc_p80max_yasa_ST_10_norm_reg_ffbs*100:.0f}} % for single parameters), and distribution-free limits need at least 19 nights at α = 0.10. We therefore suggest at least 20 locally scored nights, recognising that even 30 nights left a non-negligible risk of low realised coverage in the small ST cohort ({{h_loc_p80_yasa_ST_30_norm_reg_ffbs*100:.0f}} % of repetitions).

**Limitations.** (i) Coverage is marginal, not conditional: the hardest nights were under-covered (about {{h_q1_all_Q1_lowest_accuracy_norm_reg_ffbs_mean*100:.0f}} % in the lowest-accuracy quartile), although less than with limits of agreement. (ii) Validity requires exchangeability between calibration and test nights; it failed across cohorts, and weighted conformal methods for distribution shift [@tibshirani2019; @barber2023] are a possible extension. Two nights per subject in Sleep-EDF and small calibration sets also weaken the finite-sample guarantee (some under-coverage of distribution-free limits in ST). (iii) Each night had one human scorer, so the intervals cover that scorer's values, including scorer-specific disagreement [@rosenberg2013; @dankerhopfe2009]. (iv) The cohorts were moderate in size (348 nights), adult and scored with two rule sets, both stagers were feature-based, and HMC provides no demographics; deep-learning stagers were not evaluated, although the method only needs epoch probabilities. (v) The first-order HMM ignores longer-range sleep architecture. (vi) After an internal review the primary analysis changed from the pre-specified design, in which hidden Markov components were cross-fitted over the whole cohort, to the end-to-end protocol reported here; comparators and decision thresholds were added at the same time. The pre-specified analysis (Supplementary S9) led to the same qualitative conclusions, and all deviations are documented (Supplementary S1). The protocol was held in the study repository and not registered in an external registry, and, because test nights use the model fitted on all calibration nights, validity rests on empirical coverage rather than on a finite-sample guarantee (Section 2.5).
