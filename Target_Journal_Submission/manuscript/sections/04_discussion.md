## 4. Discussion

We have shown that the epoch probabilities of an automatic stager can be turned into per-night intervals for clinical sleep parameters that achieve their nominal coverage within a cohort, for an external pretrained stager and an in-domain stager alike. Three findings have practical consequences. First, the width of a night's interval should depend on the night: scaling by the spread of posterior hypnogram samples was more efficient than a constant width with the same centre in most settings, for both stagers and all three cohorts, and against the strongest constant-width competitor—bias-corrected, distribution-free limits of agreement—it was better for TST and stage percentages, though not for REM % or sleep onset latency. Second, most of the stagers' systematic underestimation of TST was removed by recalibrating their epoch probabilities on a few locally scored nights; averaging over posterior hypnograms added little to the point estimate. Third, neither intervals nor limits of agreement established in one cohort can be assumed to hold in another; a handful of locally scored nights was enough to restore nominal coverage.

**Relation to previous work.** Epoch-level uncertainty methods [@kang2021; @fiorillo2021; @phan2022; @heremans2024; @bechny2024; @li2025conmil] answer which epochs to review; night-level scorability models [@mikkelsen2020; @ganglberger2024] answer whether a recording is likely to be scored well; Hardarson et al. [@hardarson2023] showed, at the population level, how staging errors propagate to sleep parameters. Our contribution is complementary: a per-night interval in the units clinicians use (minutes, percentage of TST) with a coverage statement that is verified empirically and guaranteed under exchangeability. Unlike the parametric limits of agreement routinely reported in validation studies, the intervals require no normality assumption, and unlike independent per-epoch sampling, FFBS respects the temporal structure of hypnograms, which is what makes latencies and WASO uncertain. Because the procedure only consumes epoch probabilities, it can be added to existing stagers without retraining.

**Where the method helps and where it does not.** The centre × scale factorial separates two ingredients that are easily confounded. The adaptive scale was beneficial almost regardless of the centre, which is the specific contribution of posterior hypnogram sampling: nights whose sampled hypnograms disagree are the nights whose parameters are uncertain. The centre, in contrast, should be chosen per parameter. The posterior mean was a good centre for N1 %, N2 % and REM latency but a biased one for REM %, SOL and long-window WASO; with strongly tempered emissions (τ ≈ 0.1), posterior samples are pulled towards the typical stage composition of the source cohort, which inflates REM % and shortens latencies. Centring on the locally calibrated plug-in and scaling by the posterior spread, an exploratory variant, avoided these biases (Supplementary Table S4). For the skewed and bounded latencies, conformalised posterior quantiles were better than the symmetric score; we report this as a post-hoc observation to be confirmed. The SC WASO failure illustrates that per-night uncertainty is only as meaningful as the parameter definition: when the analysis window extends far beyond the final awakening, a single spurious late sleep epoch changes WASO by hours, and posterior sampling makes such epochs visible rather than hiding them. In clinical PSG, which ends at lights-on, this situation is rare (HMC), but the same caution applies to long ambulatory recordings.

**Clinical use.** In practice a laboratory deploying an automatic stager would (i) score a small set of local nights manually, as recommended for validating any scoring method, (ii) use them to recalibrate the stager's probabilities and the intervals, and (iii) report each automatically scored parameter with its interval. Nights whose intervals are wide, or whose interval straddles a decision threshold (e.g. a TST of 6 h or a REM latency of 15 min for a sleep-onset REM period), are natural candidates for manual review. Our recalibration experiment suggests that about ten local nights suffice for nominal average coverage; with so few nights, conformal intervals become conservative by construction, and coverage of a particular site's future nights varies around the nominal level, so larger local calibration sets are preferable when available.

**Limitations.** (i) Coverage is marginal over nights, not conditional on a night's characteristics; subgroup coverage was close to nominal in our data but cannot be guaranteed in general. (ii) Validity requires exchangeability; under cohort shift it failed for N3 %, and our recalibration remedy needs local manual scoring. Weighted conformal methods for covariate or label shift [@tibshirani2019; @barber2023] are a possible extension. (iii) Each night had a single human scorer, so the intervals cover that scorer's values; scorer disagreement [@rosenberg2013; @dankerhopfe2009] is part of the "error" the intervals absorb, and a multi-scorer reference would yield different widths. (iv) The cohorts are moderate in size (348 nights), adult, and scored with two rule sets; the in-domain stager was feature-based, and we did not evaluate deep-learning stagers, although the method is model-agnostic. (v) HMC provides no per-subject demographics, so age-related analyses were limited to Sleep-EDF. (vi) The first-order HMM ignores longer-range sleep architecture (e.g. cycle structure); richer posterior models could narrow intervals further. (vii) The bias-corrected and calibrated-centre comparators, the centre × scale factorial and the WASO analysis were added after the first results to separate competing explanations; they made our conclusions more conservative, and all deviations from the protocol are documented (Supplementary S1). (viii) Bias-corrected limits of agreement estimate their bias on the calibration nights and are therefore only approximately valid, which showed as some under-coverage in the small ST cohort.
