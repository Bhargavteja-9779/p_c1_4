## 4. Discussion

Epoch probabilities of an automatic stager can be turned into per-night intervals for clinical sleep parameters that reach their nominal coverage within a cohort when every component is fitted on the calibration nights alone, for an external pretrained stager and an in-domain stager. Whether such intervals are more informative than the limits of agreement already used in validation studies depended on the parameter. For TST they had lower interval scores than parametric, regression-based and distribution-free limits in every cohort, significantly so against distribution-free limits, and they allowed more nights to be classified confidently at a 6-h threshold. For the other parameters they offered no consistent advantage, and for WASO in long ambulatory recordings they were worse. Neither the intervals nor any limits of agreement transferred between cohorts; calibration on locally scored nights restored average coverage, but not reliably at every site with ten nights.

**Relation to previous work.** Epoch-level uncertainty methods [@kang2021; @fiorillo2021; @phan2022; @heremans2024; @bechny2024; @li2025conmil] identify epochs to review, night-level scorability models [@mikkelsen2020; @ganglberger2024] predict whether a recording will be scored well, and Hardarson et al. [@hardarson2023] showed at the population level how staging errors propagate to sleep parameters. Posterior sampling of whole hypnograms has been used to describe the uncertainty of overnight statistics [@vangorp2023icassp; @vangorp2023jbhi], without a guarantee that the resulting ranges contain the clinical scorer's value. The present procedure adds that guarantee, in the units clinicians use, for any stager that outputs epoch probabilities, and compares it head-to-head with the agreement statistics that validation studies report [@svensson2024; @beau2025; @kaneda2026].

**Why the gain was specific to TST.** The adaptive scale did what it was designed to do: at a fixed centre, widths proportional to the posterior spread were more efficient than constant widths for almost every parameter, and the advantage over limits of agreement was largest on the nights that were hardest to score. Whether this translated into an advantage over limits of agreement depended on how much of a parameter's error the posterior spread explains. TST errors accumulate over the whole night from many ambiguous sleep–wake epochs, which is exactly what sampled hypnograms vary. Errors in stage percentages are dominated by systematic, night-to-night differences in how a stager and a scorer draw stage boundaries (for example N3 under different rule sets), which a posterior given the stager's own probabilities cannot see; the posterior spread then adds little to a well-chosen constant width. Latencies depend on single transitions and have skewed error distributions; for sleep onset latency, conformalised posterior quantiles were better than the symmetric score (Supplementary S3). Regression-based limits of agreement, which model level-dependent bias, achieved competitive interval scores for these parameters but did not reach nominal coverage.

**The WASO failure and the choice of centre.** In the SC recordings the fixed 10-h window extends far beyond the final awakening; a single spurious sleep epoch late in a sampled hypnogram converts this wake into WASO, so the posterior mean over-estimated WASO by {{h_pe_lgbm_sc_s0_w_SC_WASO_ffbs_mean_bias:.0f}}–{{h_pe_yasa_w_SC_WASO_ffbs_mean_bias:.0f}} min and the intervals became wide. This is a property of the parameter definition as much as of the method: per-night uncertainty is only meaningful for parameters that are stable to isolated epoch errors, and in clinical PSG ending at lights-on (HMC) the problem was absent. More generally, the pre-specified configuration was not optimal: removing the regulariser of the score lowered the interval score by about {{(1-1/h_meanratio_yasa_norm_ffbs)*100:.0f}} % on average, and the calibrated plug-in was an equally good centre for several parameters. We did not tune the method on these results; parameter-specific choices would need confirmation in independent data.

**Implications for practice.** A laboratory deploying an automatic stager can (i) score a set of local nights manually, as recommended for validating any scoring method; (ii) use them to recalibrate the stager's probabilities, which by itself removed most of the TST bias; and (iii) report TST with a per-night interval, reviewing nights whose interval is wide or straddles a decision threshold. For parameters other than TST, distribution-free limits of agreement, calibrated on the same local nights, were a simpler and equally informative alternative. Ten local nights restored average coverage, but a site's realised coverage still fell below 80 % in up to {{h_loc_p80_yasa_ST_10_norm_reg_ffbs*100:.0f}} % of our repetitions, and distribution-free limits need at least 19 nights at α = 0.10. We therefore suggest at least 20 locally scored nights, recognising that even 30 nights left a non-negligible risk of low realised coverage in the small ST cohort ({{h_loc_p80_yasa_ST_30_norm_reg_ffbs*100:.0f}} % of repetitions).

**Limitations.** (i) Coverage is marginal, not conditional: the hardest nights were under-covered (about {{h_q1_all_Q1_lowest_accuracy_norm_reg_ffbs_mean*100:.0f}} % in the lowest-accuracy quartile), although less than with limits of agreement. (ii) Validity requires exchangeability between calibration and test nights; it failed across cohorts, and weighted conformal methods for distribution shift [@tibshirani2019; @barber2023] are a possible extension. Within-subject correlation (two nights per subject in Sleep-EDF) combined with small calibration sets also weakens the finite-sample guarantee, which showed as some under-coverage of distribution-free limits in ST. (iii) Each night had one human scorer, so the intervals cover that scorer's values, including scorer-specific disagreement [@rosenberg2013; @dankerhopfe2009]. (iv) The cohorts were moderate in size (348 nights), adult and scored with two rule sets, both stagers were feature-based, and HMC provides no demographics; deep-learning stagers were not evaluated, although the method only needs epoch probabilities. (v) The first-order HMM ignores longer-range sleep architecture. (vi) The primary analysis changed after the first review from the pre-registered design, in which hidden Markov components were cross-fitted over the whole cohort, to the end-to-end protocol reported here; comparators and decision thresholds were added at the same time. The pre-registered analysis, reported in full in Supplementary S9, led to the same qualitative conclusions, and all deviations are documented (Supplementary S1).
