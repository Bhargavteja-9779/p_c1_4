## 4. Discussion

Epoch probabilities of an automatic stager can be turned into per-night intervals for clinical sleep parameters that reach their nominal marginal coverage within a cohort, relative to one scorer, when every component is fitted on the calibration nights alone. Whether they are more informative than limits of agreement depended on the parameter. For TST they had lower interval scores than every form of limits of agreement in every cohort, but much of the gain came from recalibrating the stager's probabilities: against a constant width around the recalibrated plug-in it was modest in SC and HMC and absent in ST. For the other parameters they offered no consistent advantage, and for WASO in SC they were worse.

**Relation to previous work.** Epoch-level uncertainty methods [@kang2021; @fiorillo2021; @phan2022; @heremans2024; @bechny2024; @vainikka2026; @li2025conmil] identify epochs to review, night-level scorability models [@mikkelsen2020; @ganglberger2024] predict whether a recording will be scored well. Posterior sampling of whole hypnograms has been used to describe the uncertainty of overnight statistics, including against several scorers [@vangorp2023icassp; @vangorp2023jbhi], without a coverage guarantee. The present procedure adds a calibrated coverage statement in the units clinicians use, for stagers that output epoch probabilities (here two feature-based stagers sharing YASA's features), and compares it head-to-head with the agreement statistics that validation studies report [@svensson2024; @beau2025; @kaneda2026].

**Why the gain was specific to TST.** At a fixed centre, widths proportional to the posterior spread were more efficient than constant widths in most combinations (significantly in {{h_nbetter_yasa_SCALEmu}} of 24 for YASA) and the advantage over limits of agreement was largest on the hardest nights, but the protocol's joint criterion was met against Bland–Altman limits in only {{conj_yasa_ba_loa}} and {{conj_lgbm_sc_s0_ba_loa}} of 24 combinations. TST errors accumulate over the whole night from many ambiguous sleep–wake epochs, which is what sampled hypnograms vary; consistent with this, interval width correlated with the plug-in error for TST in {{e13_spearman_met_TST}} of {{e13_spearman_n_TST}} stager–cohort combinations, but for N2 % in only {{e13_spearman_met_N2pct}} and for WASO in {{e13_spearman_met_WASO}} (N1 %, {{e13_spearman_met_N1pct}} of {{e13_spearman_n_N1pct}}, showed a correlation without consistent gain). Errors in stage percentages are plausibly dominated by systematic differences in how a stager and a scorer draw stage boundaries, which a posterior given the stager's own probabilities cannot see. Latencies depend on single transitions and have skewed error distributions (for SOL, conformalised posterior quantiles beat the symmetric score; Supplementary S3). Regression-based limits were competitive in interval score but did not reach nominal coverage.

**The WASO failure and the choice of centre.** In SC, the mechanism described in Section 3.6 made the posterior mean over-estimate WASO by {{h_pe_lgbm_sc_s0_w_SC_WASO_ffbs_mean_bias:.0f}}–{{h_pe_yasa_w_SC_WASO_ffbs_mean_bias:.0f}} min and the intervals wide. This is a property of the window and the parameter definition as much as of the method: HMC recordings end at lights-on and the problem was absent. The pre-specified configuration was also not optimal (removing the regulariser lowered the interval score by about {{(1-1/h_meanratio_yasa_norm_ffbs)*100:.0f}} %, and the calibrated plug-in was an equally good centre for several parameters); we did not tune it on these results.

**Implications for practice.** A laboratory deploying an automatic stager can (i) score local nights manually; (ii) use them to recalibrate the stager's probabilities, which by itself removed most of the TST bias; and (iii) report TST with a per-night interval and review the nights whose interval is wide or straddles a decision threshold ({{(1-h_dec_yasa_TST_norm_reg_ffbs_frac)*100:.0f}} % of nights at 6 h; {{(1-r_dec_yasa_HMC_TST_norm_reg_ffbs_frac_decided)*100:.0f}} % in HMC). A 90 % interval means that the reference value of about 90 % of nights lies inside intervals so constructed, averaged over nights and relative to one scorer; it is not a probability statement about a single night. For other parameters, distribution-free limits calibrated on the same local nights were a simpler, on average similarly informative alternative (mean ratio excluding TST {{h_meanratio_noTST_yasa_scp_signed:.2f}} and {{h_meanratio_noTST_lgbm_sc_s0_scp_signed:.2f}}). The site-level risk of low realised coverage was close to finite-sample theory (Section 3.5), so it falls only slowly with more nights, and distribution-free limits need at least 19. We suggest at least 20 locally scored nights and a periodic check of realised coverage on a rolling sample of newly scored nights, because a change of equipment, montage or population would otherwise go unnoticed.

**Limitations.** (i) Coverage is marginal, not conditional: the hardest nights were under-covered (about {{h_q1_all_Q1_lowest_accuracy_norm_reg_ffbs_mean*100:.0f}} % in the lowest-accuracy quartile, {{r_terc_yasa_HMC_wide*100:.0f}} % for TST in the widest-interval tercile in HMC), although less than with limits of agreement. (ii) Validity requires exchangeability between calibration and test nights; it was not reliably met across cohorts, and weighted conformal methods for distribution shift [@tibshirani2019; @barber2023] are a possible extension. Two nights per subject in Sleep-EDF and small calibration sets also weaken the finite-sample guarantee (some under-coverage of distribution-free limits in ST); interval-score p-values ignore calibration-set variability and are descriptive. (iii) Each night had one human scorer, so the intervals cover that scorer's values, including scorer-specific disagreement [@rosenberg2013; @dankerhopfe2009]. (iv) The cohorts were moderate in size ({{nights_total}} nights), adult and scored with two rule sets, both stagers were feature-based and shared the same features, HMC provides no demographics and the simulated artefacts were not matched to real recording failures; deep-learning stagers were not evaluated, although the method only needs epoch probabilities. (v) After an internal review the primary analysis changed from the pre-specified cohort-wide cross-fitting design to the end-to-end protocol, and comparators and decision thresholds were added; the pre-specified analysis (Supplementary S9) led to the same direction of results (the TST advantage over distribution-free limits was significant in 4 of 6 combinations), and all deviations are documented (Supplementary S1).
