## 2. Materials and methods

### 2.1. Datasets
We used three openly available polysomnography (PSG) cohorts (Table 1). Sleep-EDF Expanded [@kemp2000; @sleepedf_data] provides 153 ambulatory home recordings from 78 healthy adults aged 25–101 years (SC cohort, mostly two nights per subject) and 44 in-hospital nights from 22 adults with mild difficulty falling asleep, recorded once after placebo and once after temazepam (ST cohort). The Haaglanden Medisch Centrum (HMC) database [@alvarez2021; @hmc_data] contains 151 clinical PSGs of patients referred to a sleep centre for various sleep disorders. SC and ST were scored by Rechtschaffen and Kales rules (stages S3 and S4 merged into N3), HMC by AASM rules [@berry2017]. All data are de-identified and publicly released; this secondary analysis collected no new data.

### 2.2. Analysis windows and night-level parameters
Windows were fixed from recording metadata, not from the reference hypnogram: SC, lights-off to lights-off + 10 h (the 20-h files contain daytime naps); ST, lights-off to end of recording; HMC, the annotated lights-off and lights-on markers (all truncated at the end of the scored part). Within each window we computed eight parameters for a hypnogram *y*: total sleep time (TST), sleep onset latency from lights-off (SOL), wake after sleep onset (WASO; wake between sleep onset and the final sleep epoch), REM latency from sleep onset (REML), and the percentages of TST spent in N1, N2, N3 and REM. Epochs left unscored in the reference (movement, unknown) were counted as wake in the reference hypnogram, whereas automatic hypnograms were evaluated on all epochs, so that no reference information enters the automatic parameters. REML was analysed only for nights whose reference contained REM sleep; if a sampled or automatic hypnogram contained no REM, its REML was right-censored at the window end.

### 2.3. Automatic stagers
The method operates on the epoch-wise stage probabilities of any stager. We studied two. (i) YASA [@vallat2021], a published open-source stager trained by its authors on several thousand NSRR recordings; we used its EEG + EOG model without retraining, so every night is an external test night (SC/ST: Fpz-Cz and horizontal EOG; HMC: C4-M1 and E1-M2, the montage its authors recommend, which makes the montage change between cohorts a sensitivity analysis with frontal F4-M1). (ii) An in-domain LightGBM model trained on the same 116 YASA features of SC nights, with out-of-fold SC predictions from subject-grouped five-fold cross-validation and a model trained on all SC nights for ST and HMC (where it additionally faces a montage change). Hyper-parameters were fixed a priori; three predefined seeds were run. The stager's own hypnogram (most probable stage per epoch) defines the *plug-in* parameter estimates, i.e. current practice.

### 2.4. Posterior sampling of whole hypnograms
Let $p_t(k)$ be the stager probability of stage $k$ for epoch $t$ of a night with $N$ epochs. Probabilities were first recalibrated by bias-corrected temperature scaling [@alexandari2020],

$$\tilde p_t(k)=\operatorname{softmax}_k\left(\frac{\log p_t(k)}{T}+b_k\right) \qquad (1)$$

fitted by maximum likelihood on labelled source nights; $\pi_s$ denotes the stage distribution of those nights. Following the hybrid classifier/hidden-Markov formulation [@bourlard1994; @rabiner1989], scaled likelihoods

$$e_t(k)=\left[\frac{\tilde p_t(k)}{\pi_s(k)}\right]^{\tau} \qquad (2)$$

serve as emission scores of a first-order Markov chain with transition matrix $A$ and initial distribution $p_0$ estimated from the source hypnograms (add-one smoothing). Given the night's signals, the posterior over complete hypnograms is then

$$P(y_1,\dots,y_N)\propto p_0(y_1)\,e_1(y_1)\prod_{t=2}^{N} A(y_{t-1},y_t)\,e_t(y_t) \qquad (3)$$

Because most stagers already use neighbouring epochs as context (YASA uses rolling-window features), multiplying their outputs along the chain would count the same evidence repeatedly; the emission temperature $\tau \in [0.02, 1.5]$ absorbs this and was fitted on source nights by maximising the smoothed posterior log-likelihood of the reference stages. We drew $M = 1000$ hypnograms per night exactly from (3) by forward-filtering backward-sampling (FFBS) [@carter1994; @fruhwirth1994; @chib1996]: forward probabilities $\alpha_t(k) \propto e_t(k)\sum_j \alpha_{t-1}(j)A(j,k)$, then $y_N \sim \alpha_N$ and, given $y_{t+1}$, $y_t \sim \alpha_t(j)A(j,y_{t+1})$ for $t=N-1,\dots,1$. Every parameter $\theta$ was computed on every sample, giving a per-night posterior mean $\hat\mu$ and standard deviation $\hat\sigma$ (and quantiles). Unlike independent per-epoch sampling, FFBS preserves the temporal correlation of stage errors; posterior sampling of whole hypnograms has been proposed with generative models [@vangorp2023jbhi], and the HMM provides a post-hoc analogue for any stager.

### 2.5. Conformal calibration of per-night intervals
Posterior spreads from (3) are not calibrated: they describe uncertainty given the model, not the stager's systematic errors. We therefore used split conformal prediction [@vovk2005; @lei2018; @angelopoulos2023]. For $n$ labelled calibration nights with reference values $\theta_i$ we computed normalised scores

$$s_i=\frac{|\theta_i-\hat\mu_i|}{\hat\sigma_i+\bar\sigma} \qquad (4)$$

where $\bar\sigma$ is the median of $\hat\sigma$ over the calibration and test nights (a regulariser that stops nights with near-zero posterior spread from dominating; $\hat\sigma$ requires no labels, so computing $\bar\sigma$ over all nights keeps the score a symmetric function of the data). With $\hat q$ the $k$-th smallest score, where $k$ is $(n+1)(1-\alpha)$ rounded up, the interval for a new night is

$$C(x)=\left[\hat\mu-\hat q\,(\hat\sigma+\bar\sigma),\;\hat\mu+\hat q\,(\hat\sigma+\bar\sigma)\right] \qquad (5)$$

intersected with the feasible range (≥ 0 min; 0–100 %). If the score function is fixed independently of the calibration nights and calibration and test nights are exchangeable, $P\{\theta \in C(x)\} \ge 1-\alpha$ in finite samples, whatever the stager or the HMM [@vovk2005; @lei2018]. Our main design departs from this: calibration scores come from models cross-fitted within the calibration set, whereas test nights use the model fitted on all calibration nights. This is the K-fold analogue of the jackknife of Barber et al. [@barber2021], for which that paper gives no distribution-free guarantee (CV+ applies the fold models to test nights and guarantees coverage $\ge 1-2\alpha$ minus a small finite-sample term); validity is therefore an empirical property, and coverage is reported throughout. The method, its score and $\alpha = 0.10$ were fixed in a protocol (held in the study repository, not externally registered) written before any interval was computed; the one later change, the definition of $\bar\sigma$, is documented in Supplementary S1.

### 2.6. Comparators and ablations
All comparators used the same calibration nights: (a) Bland–Altman limits of agreement around the plug-in estimate [@bland1986], computed as a prediction interval (bias ± $t_{n-1}$·SD·$\sqrt{1+1/n}$); (b) regression-based, heteroscedastic limits of agreement after Bland and Altman [@bland1999], in which bias and mean absolute residual are linear in the automatic value (which replaces the pair mean as regressor because the reference is unknown at prediction time); (c) distribution-free limits of agreement (exact for exchangeable nights): the plug-in plus conformal lower and upper quantiles (each at $\alpha/2$) of the signed residual; (d) constant-width conformal intervals [@lei2018] around the raw plug-in, the plug-in of the locally recalibrated probabilities, or $\hat\mu$; (e) conformalised quantile regression (CQR) [@romano2019] on night-level features, with linear quantile regression or applied to the FFBS 5th/95th percentiles; (f) normalised conformal intervals with mean epoch entropy as scale; (g) raw FFBS percentile intervals without conformal calibration (Supplementary S1). Crossing the centres in (d) with the FFBS-SD scale of (4) separates the effect of the adaptive scale from that of the centre; scale-only ablations kept the centre $\hat\mu$ and replaced the scale by the SD of independent per-epoch samples or of FFBS samples with $\tau = 1$, or removed the regulariser $\bar\sigma$. Comparators (b)–(d) beyond the pre-specified ones, the factorial and the scale-only ablations were added after the first results (Supplementary S1).

### 2.7. Evaluation design

*Primary within-cohort analysis (end-to-end protocol).* For each stager and cohort, subjects were split 50 times into calibration and test halves. Every label-dependent component—probability recalibration, $\pi_s$, $A$, $p_0$, $\tau$ and the conformal quantile—was fitted on the calibration subjects only; calibration-night scores came from five-fold cross-fitting within the calibration half, and test-night summaries from the model fitted on the whole half, as a laboratory would deploy the method. The pre-specified design (Supplementary S9), in which HMM components were cross-fitted over the whole cohort (so that a test night's model could use the labels of other test-half subjects) and 500 splits plus leave-one-subject-out calibration were drawn, was also used for the adaptivity, flagging, subgroup, robustness, Mondrian, sensitivity (M, calibration size, stager seeds), error-analysis and emission-temperature results and for Fig. 1; these passages are marked “pre-specified design”.

*Difficult nights.* Because marginal coverage can hide under-coverage of hard recordings, coverage was also computed in the quartile of nights with the lowest plug-in epoch accuracy (a label-dependent quantity used only for evaluation), and interval scores were compared separately for nights above and below the median accuracy.

*Local deployment.* On ST and HMC, $n_t \in \{10, 20, 30\}$ nights (whole subjects; 50 repetitions) were the only labelled data; all components were fitted on them and the remaining nights tested. We report coverage, width, the probability that a site's realised coverage falls below 80 %, and the TST bias of the recalibrated plug-in.

*Transportability.* Intervals calibrated on all SC nights were applied unchanged to ST and HMC; for YASA this was repeated with the frontal F4-M1 derivation on HMC to separate montage from population and scoring-rule (R&K versus AASM [@moser2009]) differences.

*Robustness.* EEG and EOG of test nights were corrupted with Gaussian noise (0.5 and 1.0 × the channel's robust SD) or movement-artefact drifts in 10 % or 25 % of epochs, while calibration nights stayed clean (YASA; 200 splits of the pre-specified design).

*Clinical use.* For illustrative decision thresholds chosen by the authors (TST < 6 h, SOL > 30 min, WASO > 30 min, REM latency ≤ 15 min; added after the first results and fixed before this analysis was run) we counted the nights whose interval lay entirely on one side of the threshold ("confident decisions") and the accuracy of these decisions against the reference. We also asked whether interval width identifies nights with clinically relevant plug-in errors (|ΔTST| > 30 min, |ΔREML| > 30 min, |ΔN3 %| > 5 percentage points) better than label-free alternatives (epoch entropy, maximum probability, raw-minus-recalibrated plug-in difference), and compared point estimates by bias and mean absolute error (ICC(A,1) [@mcgraw1996] in Supplementary S9).

### 2.8. Statistical analysis
Validity was measured by coverage, the fraction of test nights whose interval contained the reference value. Efficiency was measured by the interval score [@gneiting2007], $\mathrm{IS}=(u-l)+\tfrac{2}{\alpha}(l-\theta)\mathbb{1}\{\theta<l\}+\tfrac{2}{\alpha}(\theta-u)\mathbb{1}\{\theta>u\}$ for an interval $[l,u]$, which rewards narrow intervals and penalises misses in proportion to their size (lower is better). Subjects were the unit of analysis. In the primary analysis, each subject's interval scores were averaged over the repetitions in which the subject was tested; the proposed method was compared with each comparator by a two-sided sign-flip permutation test of the mean per-subject difference (20,000 permutations), which tests the reported ratio of mean interval scores, with subject-bootstrap 95 % confidence intervals for the ratio and Holm adjustment across comparators and parameters within each cohort and stager; the tests treat each calibration set as fixed, so p-values are conditional on it. Coverage is reported as the mean over repetitions with the 2.5th–97.5th percentile range. AUROC confidence intervals used 1000 subject-level cluster-bootstrap resamples. The protocol's success criteria were coverage within ±3 percentage points of nominal and, for adaptivity, a lower interval score than constant-width intervals (Bland–Altman and split-conformal absolute-residual limits; Holm-adjusted p < 0.05) together with a positive width–error Spearman correlation whose 95 % confidence interval excludes zero; both conditions are reported separately and jointly (Section 3.3). Scale comparisons at fixed centres formed a separate Holm family, and the AUROC bootstrap used 1000 instead of 2000 resamples (Supplementary S1).

### 2.9. Software, reproducibility and use of AI
Analyses used Python 3.13, YASA 0.8.1, LightGBM 4.7, scikit-learn 1.9 and NumPy/Numba on a 4-core CPU without GPU. A pipeline regenerates the results, tables and figures from the public data (large per-night files are regenerated rather than stored), and the numbers in the Results and tables are read from machine-generated files. A generative AI system (Claude, Anthropic) was used to search the literature, write and run the analysis code and draft the manuscript; all analyses are reproducible from the released code, and the authors verified the content (see Declaration).
