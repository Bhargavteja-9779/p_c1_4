## 3. Results

### 3.1. Cohorts and stagers
The analysis included {{nights_total}} nights from {{subjects_total}} subjects ({{epochs_total:,}} scored epochs; Table 1). Mean per-night Cohen's κ of the external YASA stager was {{kappa_yasa_SC:.2f}} (SC), {{kappa_yasa_ST:.2f}} (ST) and {{kappa_yasa_HMC:.2f}} (HMC); the in-domain LightGBM stager reached {{kappa_lgbm_sc_s0_SC:.2f}} on SC (out-of-fold) but only {{kappa_lgbm_sc_s0_HMC:.2f}} on HMC, where it also faced a montage change. The fitted emission temperature was τ = {{tau_median:.2f}} (range {{tau_min:.2f}}–{{tau_max:.2f}}) across folds and stagers, i.e. both stagers' outputs had to be strongly down-weighted along the chain, consistent with their built-in temporal context. ### 3.2. Within-cohort validity
Within each cohort, the proposed 90 % intervals covered the reference value in {{cov_min_yasa*100:.1f}}–{{cov_max_yasa*100:.1f}} % of test nights for YASA and {{cov_min_lgbm_sc_s0*100:.1f}}–{{cov_max_lgbm_sc_s0*100:.1f}} % for LightGBM across all 24 cohort–parameter combinations (500 splits; Table 2), meeting the pre-specified ±3 percentage-point criterion; the other conformal comparators with exact guarantees behaved similarly (Supplementary Table S2). Parametric Bland–Altman limits of agreement fell as low as {{cov_min_yasa_ba_loa*100:.1f}} % (YASA) and {{cov_min_lgbm_sc_s0_ba_loa*100:.1f}} % (LightGBM), and their bias-corrected distribution-free counterpart, whose bias is estimated on the calibration nights, to {{cov_min_yasa_scp_abs_bc*100:.1f}} % and {{cov_min_lgbm_sc_s0_scp_abs_bc*100:.1f}} %, mostly in the small ST cohort. Uncalibrated posterior percentile intervals covered only {{bayes_oth_min*100:.0f}}–{{bayes_oth_max*100:.0f}} % of reference values for TST, WASO and stage percentages while over-covering the latencies ({{bayes_lat_min*100:.0f}}–{{bayes_lat_max*100:.0f}} %; YASA), and intervals from independent per-epoch sampling covered as little as {{cov_min_yasa_ind_pct*100:.0f}} %: posterior spread alone misstates the error of night-level parameters, and ignoring the temporal correlation of epoch errors makes this much worse. Coverage of the proposed intervals was close to nominal across subgroups of age, sex and drug condition ({{subgroup_cov_min_noacc*100:.0f}}–{{subgroup_cov_max_noacc*100:.0f}} % in subgroups with ≥40 nights; Supplementary Table S9) but not across nights of different difficulty: in the quartile of nights with the lowest plug-in accuracy it was {{q1cov_min_proposed*100:.0f}}–{{q1cov_max_proposed*100:.0f}} % (distribution-free limits of agreement: {{q1cov_min_dfloa*100:.0f}}–{{q1cov_max_dfloa*100:.0f}} %), and in the best quartile {{q4cov_min_proposed*100:.0f}}–{{q4cov_max_proposed*100:.0f}} % ({{q4cov_min_dfloa*100:.0f}}–{{q4cov_max_dfloa*100:.0f}} %). In an exploratory analysis, calibrating separately for nights above and below the median mean epoch entropy—a label-free difficulty proxy (Mondrian calibration)—raised worst-quartile coverage in {{mondrian_n_improved}} of {{mondrian_n}} cohort–stager combinations (to {{q1cov_min_mondrian_entropy*100:.0f}}–{{q1cov_max_mondrian_entropy*100:.0f}} %) with unchanged overall coverage ({{allcov_min_mondrian_entropy*100:.1f}}–{{allcov_max_mondrian_entropy*100:.1f}} %; Supplementary S17).

### 3.3. Efficiency: the contribution of the centre and of the adaptive scale
Interval efficiency depended on two separable choices, the centre of the interval and its width (Fig. 2; Supplementary Tables S2–S4). *Scale:* holding the centre fixed, scaling the width by the FFBS posterior standard deviation lowered the mean interval score relative to a constant width in {{fact_lower_yasa_mu}} of {{fact_n_yasa_mu}} cohort–parameter combinations with the posterior-mean centre, {{fact_lower_yasa_cal}} with the calibrated plug-in centre and {{fact_lower_yasa_raw}} with the raw plug-in centre for YASA ({{fact_lower_lgbm_sc_s0_mu}}, {{fact_lower_lgbm_sc_s0_cal}} and {{fact_lower_lgbm_sc_s0_raw}} for LightGBM; median interval-score ratios {{fact_median_ratio_min:.2f}}–{{fact_median_ratio_max:.2f}}). In the paired leave-one-subject-out analysis these reductions were significant (Holm-adjusted p < 0.05) in {{fact_better_yasa_mu}}, {{fact_better_yasa_cal}} and {{fact_better_yasa_raw}} combinations, against {{fact_worse_yasa_mu}}, {{fact_worse_yasa_cal}} and {{fact_worse_yasa_raw}} significant increases (YASA; LightGBM: {{fact_better_lgbm_sc_s0_mu}}, {{fact_better_lgbm_sc_s0_cal}}, {{fact_better_lgbm_sc_s0_raw}} versus {{fact_worse_lgbm_sc_s0_mu}}, {{fact_worse_lgbm_sc_s0_cal}}, {{fact_worse_lgbm_sc_s0_raw}}). *Centre:* in most settings, the posterior mean was the better centre for N1 %, N2 % and REM latency, and the locally calibrated plug-in for REM %, SOL and WASO (Supplementary Table S4; Section 3.6).

Against the strongest constant-width competitor—bias-corrected, distribution-free limits of agreement—the pre-specified method had a significantly lower interval score in {{n_sig_better_vs_scp_abs_bc_yasa}} and a significantly higher one in {{n_sig_worse_vs_scp_abs_bc_yasa}} of 24 combinations for YASA ({{n_sig_better_vs_scp_abs_bc_lgbm_sc_s0}} and {{n_sig_worse_vs_scp_abs_bc_lgbm_sc_s0}} for LightGBM; Table 2). The largest gains were for TST (interval-score ratios {{isratio_yasa_SC_TST_scp_abs_bc:.2f}}, {{isratio_yasa_ST_TST_scp_abs_bc:.2f}} and {{isratio_yasa_HMC_TST_scp_abs_bc:.2f}} in SC, ST and HMC) and N2 %; the significant losses concerned {{losses_vs_dfloa_yasa}} for YASA and {{losses_vs_dfloa_lgbm_sc_s0}} for LightGBM, parameters for which the posterior-mean centre was poor. Parametric Bland–Altman limits performed similarly to their distribution-free counterpart when they were valid (Table 2). The ablations showed that the pre-specified configuration was not the best variant for every parameter (Supplementary Table S4; ratios > 1 favour the pre-specified method). Removing the regulariser $\bar\sigma$ lowered the interval score modestly for most parameters (median ratio {{ablation_median_ratio_yasa_norm_ffbs:.2f}}); an emission temperature of τ = 1 instead of the fitted value improved SOL and WASO (median ratios {{ablation_ratio_yasa_norm_reg_ffbs1_SOL:.2f}} and {{ablation_ratio_yasa_norm_reg_ffbs1_WASO:.2f}}) but worsened N2 % and N3 % ({{ablation_ratio_yasa_norm_reg_ffbs1_N2pct:.2f}} and {{ablation_ratio_yasa_norm_reg_ffbs1_N3pct:.2f}}); conformalised FFBS quantiles (CQR) were better for the skewed latencies ({{ablation_ratio_yasa_cqr_ffbs_SOL:.2f}} for SOL, {{ablation_ratio_yasa_cqr_ffbs_REML:.2f}} for REML); sampling epochs independently instead of by FFBS worsened the latencies ({{ablation_ratio_yasa_norm_reg_ind_REML:.2f}} for REML, {{ablation_ratio_yasa_norm_reg_ind_SOL:.2f}} for SOL) but was as good or better for N1 % and REM % ({{ablation_ratio_yasa_norm_reg_ind_N1pct:.2f}} and {{ablation_ratio_yasa_norm_reg_ind_REMpct:.2f}}); and scaling by mean epoch entropy was worse for the latencies ({{ablation_ratio_yasa_norm_entropy_reg_SOL:.2f}} for SOL, {{ablation_ratio_yasa_norm_entropy_reg_REML:.2f}} for REML) and similar for stage percentages (all YASA; LightGBM in Supplementary Table S4). Results were stable across stager seeds (maximum coverage range {{seed_max_cov_range*100:.1f}} percentage points across {{n_seeds}} seeds) and α levels (Supplementary Tables S5–S6).

### 3.4. Point estimates
The stagers' own hypnograms systematically underestimated TST (YASA bias {{pe_yasa_SC_TST_plug_in_bias:.1f}}, {{pe_yasa_ST_TST_plug_in_bias:.1f}} and {{pe_yasa_HMC_TST_plug_in_bias:.1f}} min in SC, ST and HMC; Table 3). Most of this bias was removed by recalibrating the epoch probabilities on the labelled calibration nights (calibrated plug-in: {{pe_yasa_SC_TST_plug_in_calibrated_bias:.1f}}, {{pe_yasa_ST_TST_plug_in_calibrated_bias:.1f}} and {{pe_yasa_HMC_TST_plug_in_calibrated_bias:.1f}} min), which also halved the mean absolute error in HMC ({{pe_yasa_HMC_TST_plug_in_mae:.0f}} to {{pe_yasa_HMC_TST_plug_in_calibrated_mae:.0f}} min). Averaging over posterior hypnograms added little beyond calibration: the posterior-mean TST error was similar to that of the calibrated plug-in in HMC and ST (MAE {{pe_yasa_HMC_TST_ffbs_mean_mae:.0f}} and {{pe_yasa_ST_TST_ffbs_mean_mae:.0f}} min) and somewhat smaller in SC ({{pe_yasa_SC_TST_ffbs_mean_mae:.0f}} versus {{pe_yasa_SC_TST_plug_in_calibrated_mae:.0f}} min). The posterior mean was worse than the calibrated plug-in for REM %, SOL and REM latency and, markedly, for WASO in SC (bias {{pe_yasa_SC_WASO_ffbs_mean_bias:.0f}} min). Findings for LightGBM were similar (Supplementary Table S8).

### 3.5. Transportability and local recalibration
Intervals calibrated on SC did not transfer reliably (Fig. 4; Supplementary Table S13). Coverage remained acceptable on ST for most parameters but collapsed for N3 % on HMC ({{x_cov_yasa_HMC_N3pct_norm_reg_ffbs*100:.0f}} % for the proposed method, {{x_cov_yasa_HMC_N3pct_ba_loa*100:.0f}} % for Bland–Altman and {{x_cov_yasa_HMC_N3pct_scp_abs_bc*100:.0f}} % for distribution-free limits of agreement with YASA); the in-domain LightGBM stager, which additionally faced a montage change, lost coverage for several parameters on HMC, including TST ({{x_cov_lgbm_sc_s0_HMC_TST_norm_reg_ffbs*100:.0f}} %). The cause was a reversal of the stager's N3 bias between cohorts (plug-in bias {{bias_yasa_SC_N3pct:+.1f}} percentage points in SC versus {{bias_yasa_HMC_N3pct:+.1f}} in HMC; Supplementary Table S10), which no interval calibrated on SC can anticipate; methods that learn and correct the SC bias were hurt most, whereas a constant-width interval around the uncorrected plug-in kept {{x_cov_yasa_HMC_N3pct_scp_abs*100:.0f}} %. Calibrating instead on a small number of manually scored target nights restored nominal coverage: with ten HMC nights, mean coverage was {{fs_cov_yasa_HMC_target_10_norm_reg_ffbs*100:.1f}} % for the proposed method versus {{fs_cov_yasa_HMC_target_10_ba_loa*100:.1f}} % for Bland–Altman and {{fs_cov_yasa_HMC_target_10_scp_abs_bc*100:.1f}} % for distribution-free limits, and with ten ST nights {{fs_cov_yasa_ST_target_10_norm_reg_ffbs*100:.1f}} % versus {{fs_cov_yasa_ST_target_10_ba_loa*100:.1f}} % and {{fs_cov_yasa_ST_target_10_scp_abs_bc*100:.1f}} %. Coverage at an individual site nevertheless varied: with ten calibration nights, the realised coverage of the remaining nights fell below 80 % in {{fs_p80_yasa_HMC_10_norm_reg_ffbs*100:.0f}} % (HMC) and {{fs_p80_yasa_ST_10_norm_reg_ffbs*100:.0f}} % (ST) of repetitions for the proposed method, versus {{fs_p80_yasa_HMC_10_ba_loa*100:.0f}} % and {{fs_p80_yasa_ST_10_ba_loa*100:.0f}} % for Bland–Altman limits (median over parameters; Supplementary S17). Pooling SC and target nights did not fully correct the HMC shift ({{fs_cov_yasa_HMC_pooled_50_norm_reg_ffbs*100:.1f}} % with 50 target nights).

### 3.6. Error analysis
Non-covered nights had lower plug-in accuracy (median {{nc_yasa_acc_plugin_med_missed:.2f}} versus {{nc_yasa_acc_plugin_med_cov:.2f}}; Mann–Whitney p < 0.05 in {{nc_yasa_acc_plugin_nsig}} of {{nc_yasa_acc_plugin_ntests}} cohort–parameter comparisons) and higher mean epoch entropy (p < 0.05 in {{nc_yasa_mean_entropy_nsig}} of {{nc_yasa_mean_entropy_ntests}}; YASA, Supplementary Table S11). The WASO failure in SC had a specific mechanism: the 10-h SC window contains long periods of wake after the final awakening (median {{waso_postwake_SC:.0f}} min, versus {{waso_postwake_HMC:.0f}} min in HMC), and a single spurious sleep epoch late in a sampled hypnogram moves the final awakening and converts this wake into WASO. On average {{waso_fraclate_SC*100:.0f}} % of samples per SC night placed the final awakening more than 60 min after the reference ({{waso_fraclate_HMC*100:.0f}} % in HMC), and this fraction correlated with the posterior-mean WASO error (Spearman ρ = {{waso_rho_SC:.2f}}).

### 3.7. Robustness to signal corruption
With calibration on clean nights and corrupted test nights, the coverage of all methods decreased as corruption increased (Fig. 5; Supplementary Table S12). No method remained valid under strong additive noise. The adaptive intervals degraded less because their width responded to the corrupted signal: for TST, coverage under strong noise fell to {{rob_cov_gauss10_TST_norm_reg_ffbs*100:.0f}} % for the proposed intervals, which widened from {{rob_width_clean_TST_norm_reg_ffbs:.0f}} to {{rob_width_gauss10_TST_norm_reg_ffbs:.0f}} min, but to {{rob_cov_gauss10_TST_scp_abs_bc*100:.0f}} % for distribution-free limits of agreement, whose width cannot change; with artefacts in 25 % of epochs, TST coverage was {{rob_cov_art25_TST_norm_reg_ffbs*100:.0f}} % versus {{rob_cov_art25_TST_scp_abs_bc*100:.0f}} %.

### 3.8. Flagging nights with large errors and computational cost
The width of a night's interval identified nights with clinically relevant plug-in errors in REM latency better than mean epoch entropy (AUROC {{auroc_yasa_SC_REML_proposed_width:.2f}} vs {{auroc_yasa_SC_REML_mean_entropy:.2f}} in SC and {{auroc_yasa_HMC_REML_proposed_width:.2f}} vs {{auroc_yasa_HMC_REML_mean_entropy:.2f}} in HMC, YASA), was modestly better for N3 % and comparable for TST (Fig. 3; Supplementary Table S14). Overall discrimination was moderate (AUROC mostly 0.6–0.8). Posterior sampling (M = 1000) and parameter computation took a median of {{eff_ffbs_s_M1000+eff_params_s_M1000:.2f}} s per night on one CPU core, compared with {{eff_stager_s:.1f}} s for YASA feature extraction and inference, and the conformal step {{eff_conformal_s*1000:.1f}} ms (Supplementary Table S15).
