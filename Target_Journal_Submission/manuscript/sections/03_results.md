## 3. Results

### 3.1. Cohorts and stagers
The analysis included {{nights_total}} nights from {{subjects_total}} subjects ({{epochs_total:,}} scored epochs; Table 1). Mean per-night Cohen's κ of the external YASA stager was {{kappa_yasa_SC:.2f}} (SC), {{kappa_yasa_ST:.2f}} (ST) and {{kappa_yasa_HMC:.2f}} (HMC); the in-domain LightGBM stager reached {{kappa_lgbm_sc_s0_SC:.2f}} on SC (out-of-fold) but only {{kappa_lgbm_sc_s0_HMC:.2f}} on HMC, where it also faced a montage change. The fitted emission temperature was τ = {{tau_median:.2f}} (range {{tau_min:.2f}}–{{tau_max:.2f}}) across folds and stagers, i.e. both stagers' outputs had to be strongly down-weighted along the chain, consistent with their built-in temporal context. Fig. 1 illustrates the procedure on one HMC night.

### 3.2. Within-cohort validity
Within each cohort, the proposed 90 % intervals covered the reference value in {{cov_min_yasa*100:.1f}}–{{cov_max_yasa*100:.1f}} % of test nights for YASA and {{cov_min_lgbm_sc_s0*100:.1f}}–{{cov_max_lgbm_sc_s0*100:.1f}} % for LightGBM across all 24 cohort–parameter combinations (500 splits; Table 2), meeting the pre-specified ±3 percentage-point criterion; the other conformal comparators behaved similarly (Supplementary Table S2). Parametric Bland–Altman limits of agreement fell as low as {{cov_min_yasa_ba_loa*100:.1f}} % (YASA) and {{cov_min_lgbm_sc_s0_ba_loa*100:.1f}} % (LightGBM), mostly in the small ST cohort. Uncalibrated posterior percentile intervals covered only {{cov_min_yasa_bayes_pct*100:.0f}}–{{cov_max_yasa_bayes_pct*100:.0f}} % (YASA), and intervals from independent per-epoch sampling as little as {{cov_min_yasa_ind_pct*100:.0f}} %: posterior spread alone grossly understates the error of night-level parameters, and ignoring the temporal correlation of epoch errors makes this much worse. Coverage of the proposed intervals was similar across subgroups of age, sex, drug condition and plug-in accuracy (range {{subgroup_cov_min*100:.0f}}–{{subgroup_cov_max*100:.0f}} % in subgroups with ≥40 nights; Supplementary Table S9).

### 3.3. Efficiency and adaptivity
Gains in interval score over constant-width intervals were parameter-dependent (Table 2; Fig. 2). In the LOSO paired analysis, the proposed intervals had a significantly lower interval score than constant-width conformal intervals in {{n_sig_better_vs_const_yasa}} of 24 cohort–parameter combinations and a significantly higher one in {{n_sig_worse_vs_const_yasa}} for YASA ({{n_sig_better_vs_const_lgbm_sc_s0}} and {{n_sig_worse_vs_const_lgbm_sc_s0}} for LightGBM; Holm-adjusted). The largest and most consistent gains were for TST, with interval-score ratios of {{isratio_yasa_SC_TST_scp_abs:.2f}} (SC), {{isratio_yasa_ST_TST_scp_abs:.2f}} (ST) and {{isratio_yasa_HMC_TST_scp_abs:.2f}} (HMC) for YASA, and for N2 % and N3 % in most settings. Against Bland–Altman limits of agreement the proposed intervals were better in {{n_sig_better_vs_ba_yasa}} and worse in {{n_sig_worse_vs_ba_yasa}} combinations (YASA). There was no consistent gain for REM %, SOL was worse in HMC and ST, and WASO was worse in the SC cohort (ratio {{isratio_yasa_SC_WASO_scp_abs:.2f}}; Section 3.6).

Part of the TST gain came from a better centre rather than from adaptivity: a constant-width interval centred on the posterior mean reduced the interval score by itself, whereas centring on the locally calibrated plug-in did so less (Supplementary Table S4). Among adaptive variants, replacing FFBS by independent per-epoch sampling widened intervals for time-structured parameters (median interval-score ratio to the proposed method {{ablation_ratio_yasa_norm_reg_ind_REML:.2f}} for REML and {{ablation_ratio_yasa_norm_reg_ind_WASO:.2f}} for WASO, YASA) while being comparable for stage percentages; fixing τ = 1 gave mixed results (median ratio {{ablation_median_ratio_yasa_norm_reg_ffbs1:.2f}}); and conformalising the FFBS quantiles (CQR) instead of the symmetric normalised score was better for the skewed latencies (median ratio {{ablation_ratio_yasa_cqr_ffbs_SOL:.2f}} for SOL and {{ablation_ratio_yasa_cqr_ffbs_REML:.2f}} for REML). Scaling by mean epoch entropy matched the proposed scale for stage percentages but not for REML or SOL. Results were stable across stager seeds (maximum coverage range {{seed_max_cov_range*100:.1f}} percentage points across {{n_seeds}} seeds) and α levels (Supplementary Tables S5–S6).

### 3.4. Point estimates
The stagers' own hypnograms systematically underestimated TST (YASA bias {{pe_yasa_SC_TST_plug_in_bias:.1f}}, {{pe_yasa_ST_TST_plug_in_bias:.1f}} and {{pe_yasa_HMC_TST_plug_in_bias:.1f}} min in SC, ST and HMC; Table 3). The FFBS posterior mean almost removed this bias ({{pe_yasa_SC_TST_ffbs_mean_bias:.1f}}, {{pe_yasa_ST_TST_ffbs_mean_bias:.1f}} and {{pe_yasa_HMC_TST_ffbs_mean_bias:.1f}} min) and reduced the mean absolute error from {{pe_yasa_HMC_TST_plug_in_mae:.0f}} to {{pe_yasa_HMC_TST_ffbs_mean_mae:.0f}} min in HMC (ICC {{pe_yasa_HMC_TST_plug_in_icc:.2f}} to {{pe_yasa_HMC_TST_ffbs_mean_icc:.2f}}); local calibration alone achieved part of this improvement (calibrated plug-in bias {{pe_yasa_HMC_TST_plug_in_calibrated_bias:.1f}} min). Posterior means also improved WASO in HMC and ST and the N1 % and N2 % estimates in most settings, but were worse than the plug-in for REM %, for REM latency and, markedly, for WASO in SC (bias {{pe_yasa_SC_WASO_ffbs_mean_bias:.0f}} min).

### 3.5. Transportability and local recalibration
Intervals calibrated on SC did not transfer reliably (Fig. 4). Coverage remained acceptable on ST for most parameters but collapsed for N3 % on HMC ({{x_cov_yasa_HMC_N3pct_norm_reg_ffbs*100:.0f}} % for the proposed method, {{x_cov_yasa_HMC_N3pct_ba_loa*100:.0f}} % for Bland–Altman limits, {{x_cov_yasa_HMC_N3pct_scp_abs*100:.0f}} % for constant-width conformal intervals with YASA). The cause was a reversal of the stager's N3 bias between cohorts (plug-in bias {{bias_yasa_SC_N3pct:+.1f}} percentage points in SC versus {{bias_yasa_HMC_N3pct:+.1f}} in HMC; Supplementary Table S10), which no interval calibrated on SC can anticipate. Calibrating instead on a small number of manually scored target nights restored nominal coverage: with ten HMC nights, mean coverage was {{fs_cov_yasa_HMC_target_10_norm_reg_ffbs*100:.1f}} % for the proposed method versus {{fs_cov_yasa_HMC_target_10_ba_loa*100:.1f}} % for Bland–Altman limits, and with ten ST nights {{fs_cov_yasa_ST_target_10_norm_reg_ffbs*100:.1f}} % versus {{fs_cov_yasa_ST_target_10_ba_loa*100:.1f}} %. Pooling SC and target nights did not fully correct the HMC shift ({{fs_cov_yasa_HMC_pooled_50_norm_reg_ffbs*100:.1f}} % with 50 target nights).

### 3.6. Error analysis
Non-covered nights were concentrated among nights with low plug-in accuracy and high epoch entropy (Supplementary Table S11). The WASO failure in SC had a specific mechanism: the 10-h SC window contains long periods of wake after the final awakening (median {{waso_postwake_SC:.0f}} min, versus {{waso_postwake_HMC:.0f}} min in HMC), and a single spurious sleep epoch late in a sampled hypnogram moves the final awakening and converts this wake into WASO. On average {{waso_fraclate_SC*100:.0f}} % of samples per SC night placed the final awakening more than 60 min after the reference ({{waso_fraclate_HMC*100:.0f}} % in HMC), and this fraction correlated with the posterior-mean WASO error (Spearman ρ = {{waso_rho_SC:.2f}}).

### 3.7. Robustness to signal corruption
With calibration on clean nights and corrupted test nights, the coverage of all methods decreased as corruption increased (Fig. 5; Supplementary Table S12). For TST, the proposed intervals kept a coverage of {{rob_cov_gauss10_TST_norm_reg_ffbs*100:.0f}} % under strong additive noise versus {{rob_cov_gauss10_TST_scp_abs*100:.0f}} % for constant-width intervals, by widening from {{rob_width_clean_TST_norm_reg_ffbs:.0f}} to {{rob_width_gauss10_TST_norm_reg_ffbs:.0f}} min; under 25 % artefact epochs, coverage was {{rob_cov_art25_TST_norm_reg_ffbs*100:.0f}} % versus {{rob_cov_art25_TST_scp_abs*100:.0f}} %.

### 3.8. Flagging nights with large errors and computational cost
The width of a night's interval identified nights with clinically relevant plug-in errors in REM latency better than mean epoch entropy (AUROC {{auroc_yasa_SC_REML_proposed_width:.2f}} vs {{auroc_yasa_SC_REML_mean_entropy:.2f}} in SC and {{auroc_yasa_HMC_REML_proposed_width:.2f}} vs {{auroc_yasa_HMC_REML_mean_entropy:.2f}} in HMC, YASA), was modestly better for N3 % and comparable for TST (Fig. 3). Overall discrimination was moderate (AUROC mostly 0.6–0.8). Posterior sampling (M = 1000) and parameter computation took a median of {{eff_ffbs_s_M1000+eff_params_s_M1000:.2f}} s per night on one CPU core, compared with {{eff_stager_s:.1f}} s for YASA feature extraction and inference, and the conformal step {{eff_conformal_s*1000:.1f}} ms.
